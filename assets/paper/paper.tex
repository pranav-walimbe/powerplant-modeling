\begin{abstract}
NASA's Tropospheric Emissions: Monitoring of Pollution (TEMPO) instrument
provides hourly daytime observations of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide
(NO$_2$) across North America. These observations could support independent,
facility-level monitoring of power plant nitrogen oxides (\NOx{}) emissions,
filling gaps when continuous emissions monitoring system (CEMS) measurements fail
or facilities are not subject to hourly emissions reporting requirements.
Previous studies have estimated hourly point-source emissions
using inversion methods, which are highly sensitive to instrument
retrieval uncertainty, changing atmospheric conditions, TEMPO's coarse spatial
resolution, signals from nearby emission sources, and temporal mismatch with hourly
CEMS records. We instead formulate facility monitoring as change detection over
sequences of TEMPO observations. This allows the model to learn relative emissions behavior rather
than estimate exact emissions from noisy data. We develop a convolutional gated recurrent unit (ConvGRU) model that processes five consecutive TEMPO observations
together with meteorological inputs, features representing plume transport, and tabular features encoding time of day and seasonality to
classify power plant emission trajectories as increasing, steady, or decreasing.
On a held-out evaluation set of more than 22,000 samples covering 116 active U.S. power
plants observed between August 2023 and June 2026, the ConvGRU model achieved a
macro-F1 score of 0.56, outperforming a baseline multilayer perceptron (MLP)
trained only on the same time-derived features, which scored 0.45.
Our findings suggest that the ConvGRU model can identify signals of power plant emissions
from TEMPO observations beyond the time-derived features shared with the baseline MLP. More broadly, they indicate
that change detection may offer a more tractable framework for modeling power plant activity from TEMPO observations
than estimating absolute emissions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Based on U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) inventory data, power plants
in the U.S. electricity generation sector emitted approximately 0.74 million
short tons of \NOx{} in 2023, about 9.5\% of national
emissions~\cite{epa2026emissionsTrends}. \NOx{} emissions contribute to
ground-level ozone and secondary particulate matter, pollutants that can
inflame and damage the airways, reduce lung function, and aggravate
asthma~\cite{epa2016noxHealth}. EPA regulations require most large U.S.
fossil-fuel-fired generating units that produce electricity for sale to submit
unit-level hourly emissions data on a quarterly basis~\cite{epa2026powerMonitoring}.
However, monitoring equipment malfunctions and maintenance can make CEMS
measurements unavailable or invalid, requiring plants to report calculated
substitute values instead of direct measurements~\cite{epa2021substitute}.
Behind-the-meter generators that do not sell electricity, such as the natural gas
turbines powering SpaceXAI's Colossus 2 data center, can fall outside federal hourly
reporting requirements~\cite{gauld2026dataCenter}.
Outside the United States, comparable public, hourly, unit-level emissions data are generally unavailable;
global inventories typically provide only annual estimates~\cite{huang2026detectability}. Independent estimates could therefore
fill reporting gaps and provide timely insight into facility operations ahead of the EPA's quarterly release
of hourly emissions data.

TEMPO is an ultraviolet-visible spectrometer hosted aboard a geostationary satellite
that observes tropospheric vertical column densities (TVCDs) for various trace gases
across North America during daylight hours~\cite{zoogman2017tempo}. Earlier polar-orbiting
instruments, including the Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) and TROPOspheric Monitoring
Instrument (TROPOMI), typically observe a given location once per day; TEMPO's geostationary
view provides approximately hourly scans with finer spatial
resolution~\cite{levelt2006omi,veefkind2012tropomi,zoogman2017tempo}. Hourly sampling
captures intraday changes in emissions plumes and atmospheric transport that daily
observations can miss. However, power plant emission signals in TEMPO NO$_2$ TVCDs can be weakened
or obscured by factors including evolving meteorology and atmospheric chemistry, cloud cover,
viewing geometry and retrieval uncertainty, dilution within TEMPO's \(2.0~\mathrm{km} \times 4.75~\mathrm{km}\) spatial resolution, and interference from nearby emission sources~\cite{tempo2026userGuide,huang2026detectability,gauld2026dataCenter}.
Comparisons with CEMS records, our most reliable ground truth, introduce an
additional temporal mismatch because TEMPO observes a facility at a specific time during each hourly scan,
whereas CEMS data aggregate emissions over an entire clock hour~\cite{tempo2026userGuide,epa2026powerMonitoring}.

Satellite instruments measure atmospheric column abundance rather than emissions
directly. Inversion methods therefore infer source rates from the
spatial structure of an observed plume and estimated winds. Cross-sectional flux
(CSF) removes the background column, integrates the remaining enhancement across
transects perpendicular to the plume, and multiplies the resulting line density by
an effective wind speed. Under steady-state conditions, this flux approximates the
source emission rate. The directional derivative approach (DDA) instead combines
the wind field with spatial gradients in the observed column to estimate a local
source term, which is then integrated around the
facility~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking,sun2025hourly}.

Although promising, these methods remain sensitive to source characteristics and
observing conditions. Kuhlmann et al. applied CSF to TROPOMI NO$_2$ observations of
18 power plants in Europe and the United States, recovering seasonal \NOx{}
emissions variability. However, the number of valid overpasses varied across plants,
ranging from 38 to 170 per year depending on local cloud cover and interference
from nearby plumes, with especially sparse winter coverage. These sampling gaps, along with uncertainty in chemistry
corrections, limited the ability to resolve short-term emissions
fluctuations~\cite{kuhlmann2026temporal}. Using DDA with TEMPO observations, Sun et al. improved
correlations with CEMS at 10 of 14 power plants by accounting for changes in NO$_2$
columns between scans, although their emissions estimates remained approximately
four times lower than CEMS measurements~\cite{sun2025hourly}. Beaudry et al. estimated emissions using a rise--decay (RD) model that
separately fits the increase in NO$_2$ line density immediately downwind of the
source and its subsequent decay farther downwind. Their average emissions
estimates were within 50\% of CEMS values at 27 of 64 plants, but bias was
substantial at plants emitting less than
\(250~\mathrm{kg~NO_2~h^{-1}}\)~\cite{beaudry2026quantification}.

Machine learning (ML) studies have addressed several related point-source monitoring
tasks. Huang and Wang trained a tabular MLP to predict whether a TROPOMI observation
contained a detectable power plant NO$_2$ plume using 25 facility, meteorological,
surface, and sensor variables sampled at the plant location~\cite{huang2026detectability}.
Their models achieved F1 scores above 0.66, but detectability
ranged from below 20\% to above 60\% for plants with similar emissions. Their
interference filters also retained plants representing only 45.0\% of U.S. and 21.1\%
of global \NOx{} emissions in their datasets. Alnaim et al. used stacked long
short-term memory (LSTM) networks to predict next-day EPA-reported NO$_2$ emissions from seven-day sequences of tabular features comprising previous emissions,
TROPOMI NO$_2$ column values extracted from areas surrounding each plant,
MERRA-2 meteorology, aerosol measurements, and calendar variables~\cite{alnaim2022evaluating}. Their best single-plant LSTM
had an error near 40\%, while multi-plant experiments exceeded 55\% error and
often underestimated extreme values. Finch et al. developed a convolutional neural
network (CNN) using 6,086 manually labeled TROPOMI images, achieving greater than
90\% test accuracy for NO$_2$ plume detection. However, reliance on manual
annotation limits the scalability of label generation, and plume presence alone is not always a reliable proxy for emissions quantity
or plant activity~\cite{finch2022automated}.

The studies reviewed above do not jointly model the spatial structure and temporal
evolution of consecutive trace gas satellite rasters. We instead formulate facility
monitoring as a sequence classification task, where we classify plant emissions
trajectories as increasing, steady, or decreasing over sequences of five TEMPO
observations. This formulation takes advantage of TEMPO's hourly temporal resolution
and reduces reliance on estimating exact emissions from noisy observations
by instead learning relative emissions behavior.

To model spatial patterns and their evolution across TEMPO observations, we
consider two families of neural networks: convolutional recurrent neural networks
(CRNNs) and spatiotemporal vision transformers (ViTs). At each timestep, a CRNN
combines features from the current raster with a spatially structured hidden state
carried from the preceding timestep. Convolution operations update the hidden state
at each location by combining information from a local neighborhood in both the
current input and the previous hidden state. Two common variants are convolutional
long short-term memory (ConvLSTM) and convolutional gated recurrent unit (ConvGRU)
networks. ConvLSTM maintains separate cell and hidden states, with gates controlling
how information is retained, updated, and exposed. ConvGRU maintains a single hidden
state controlled by reset and update gates, reducing the number of parameters
relative to a comparably configured ConvLSTM~\cite{shi2015convlstm,ballas2015convgru}.
Spatiotemporal ViTs divide each raster into patches and use self-attention to combine
information across patches and timesteps. This allows the model to relate spatially
distant regions across observations without processing the sequence recurrently.
Compared with convolutional models, spatiotemporal ViTs have a weaker locality bias
and typically require more training samples to learn relationships among patches
across input timesteps~\cite{bertasius2021timesformer}.

We select ConvGRU for the balance it offers between modeling capacity
and parameter efficiency. Each input sequence contains only five timesteps, so we favor ConvGRU's simpler
memory structure over ConvLSTM's additional cell state, which increases the
parameter count and may offer limited benefit for such short sequences. The input
rasters are also relatively small ($24 \times 24$ pixels), and the spatial continuity
of power plant plumes makes local convolutional operations well suited to modeling
their evolution across observations. TEMPO's approximately three-year observational
history also limits the available training data, motivating our choice of a compact
ConvGRU that captures spatiotemporal patterns with relatively few parameters.

\section{Data Sources}

\subsection{TEMPO NO\texorpdfstring{$_2$}{2} Data}

NASA distributes TEMPO NO$_2$ retrievals in two formats: Level 2 data on the
instrument's native measurement footprints and Level 3 data regridded from each
east-to-west scan~\cite{tempo2026userGuide}. We downloaded version 4 Level 2
NO$_2$ retrievals from August 2023 through June 2026. Along with NO$_2$
TVCDs, the product provides metadata including native footprint geometry,
mirror-step observation times, main data quality flags, and effective cloud
fractions, which we later use for regridding, quality filtering, and temporal
alignment with CEMS records.

\subsection{CEMS Records}

From the EPA's Clean Air Markets Program Data (CAMPD) APIs, we downloaded hourly
emissions records for individual U.S. power plant units from August 2023 through
June 2026 and annual facility and unit attributes~\cite{epa2026powerSectorData}.
From the hourly records, we use facility and unit identifiers,
reporting date and hour, \NOx{} mass and its measurement flag, and operating time.
From the annual attributes, we use facility coordinates.

\subsection{HRRR Weather Data}

The High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) is a weather prediction model updated
hourly that covers the contiguous United States at a spatial resolution of
3~km~\cite{noaa2026hrrrDocumentation}. From NOAA's public HRRR archive,
we downloaded hourly 2~m air temperature and eastward and northward
wind components at 80~m above ground for August 2023 through
June 2026~\cite{noaa2026hrrrArchive}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{AOI Construction}

For each power plant, we define a 72~km $\times$ 72~km area of interest (AOI)
centered on the facility and divide it into a $24 \times 24$ grid of
3~km $\times$ 3~km cells. For each AOI-hour, we sum the unit-level \NOx{} mass
reported by all facilities in the AOI and discard the hour if any contributing
measurement is invalid or unavailable. We score each AOI using its median total
\NOx{} mass across hours whose mean unit operating time meets or exceeds the
AOI-specific median. We retain the highest-scoring 50\% to focus the dataset on
emissions signals more likely to be detected by TEMPO.

\subsection{TEMPO Regridding and Quality Filtering}

TEMPO Level 2 observations lie on irregular native footprints, whereas our
model uses a fixed spatial grid. We project the footprint boundaries and AOI
grid to the contiguous-U.S. Albers equal-area coordinate system. We then assign
each AOI grid cell an NO$_2$ TVCD by averaging the TEMPO footprints that
intersect it, weighted by their area of overlap. Figure~\ref{fig:tempoRegridding}
illustrates this mapping from native footprints to the fixed AOI grid.

A footprint contributes to the NO$_2$ average only when its main data quality
flag equals zero, indicating a reliable retrieval, and its effective cloud fraction does not exceed 0.20. Grid
cells without an accepted footprint overlap remain masked, and we retain a
binary mask of the original valid cells. We use bilinear interpolation to fill
masked NO$_2$ pixels for downstream modeling.

\subsection{Sample Construction}
\label{sec:sample-construction}

Each sample contains five consecutive observations of one AOI, indexed from
$t_0$ to $t_4$. We retain a sequence only when each pair of adjacent TEMPO
scans is separated by 40--70 minutes and every NO$_2$ raster has valid pixel coverage of at
least 85\%. At each timestep, we pair the TEMPO raster with
the HRRR temperature and wind rasters from the closest hour and interpolate them to the same
$24 \times 24$ raster size and spatial coverage as the NO$_2$ rasters.

Because CEMS records correspond to clock hours rather than TEMPO scan times,
we linearly interpolate the AOI-hour \NOx{} emissions between the surrounding
hourly CEMS records at each scan time. Let $E_i$ denote the interpolated emissions
at time $t_i$. For scans $t_a$ through $t_b$, we calculate the time-weighted mean
\begin{equation}
    \overline{E}_{a:b} =
    \frac{\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}(t_{i+1}-t_i)(E_i+E_{i+1})/2}
    {t_b-t_a}.
\end{equation}
We define the sequence change as
$\Delta E=\overline{E}_{2:4}-\overline{E}_{0:2}$ and use the threshold
$\theta=250~\mathrm{lb\,h^{-1}}$ to assign its category:
\begin{equation}
    c =
    \begin{cases}
        \text{increase}, & \Delta E \geq \theta, \\
        \text{steady}, & -\theta < \Delta E < \theta, \\
        \text{decrease}, & \Delta E \leq -\theta.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

We chose the threshold $\theta=250~\mathrm{lb\,h^{-1}}$ based on the balance between
valid sample counts across classes and separability between classes from larger emissions deltas,
which we visualize in Figure~\ref{fig:label-threshold-samples}.

\begin{figure}[t]

\subsection{Transport Model}

Emissions change signals in consecutive NO$_2$ rasters can be degraded by
movement and decay of the existing plume. For each pair of consecutive NO$_2$
rasters, we use a simple transport model to predict the next observation without
new emissions in the AOI.
We then subtract this prediction from the observed next raster to obtain a
transport residual, which separates plume structure explained by modeled
advection, horizontal dispersion, and chemical decay from remaining variation that may
reflect emissions changes within the AOI.

For the interval $\Delta t_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, let $N_i(\boldsymbol{x})$ and
$N_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{x})$ denote the earlier and later NO$_2$ rasters,
$\boldsymbol{u}_i(\boldsymbol{x})$ and
$\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1}(\boldsymbol{x})$ their wind fields, and $B_i$ the median
NO$_2$ value along the boundary of the earlier raster.
Using these quantities, we construct the transported prediction and residual as
follows:

\begingroup
\makeatletter
\tagsleft@true
\makeatother
\begin{align}
  \overline{\boldsymbol{u}}_i
    &= \gamma\!\left[(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{u}_i
       + \lambda\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1}\right]
       \tag{i}\label{eq:transport-wind} \\
  A_i(\boldsymbol{x})
    &= N_i\!\left(\boldsymbol{x}
       -\overline{\boldsymbol{u}}_i(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta t_i\right)
       \tag{ii}\label{eq:transport-advection} \\
  \sigma_i
    &= \sigma_0\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t_i}{1~\mathrm{h}}}
       \tag{iii}\label{eq:transport-diffusion-scale} \\
  D_i
    &= B_i+G_{\sigma_i}*(A_i-B_i)
       \tag{iv}\label{eq:transport-diffusion} \\
  \widehat{N}_{i+1}
    &= B_i+\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta t_i}{\tau}\right)(D_i-B_i)
       \tag{v}\label{eq:transport-decay} \\
  R_i
    &= N_{i+1}-\widehat{N}_{i+1}
       \tag{vi}\label{eq:transport-residual}.
\end{align}
\endgroup

We selected the values of $\lambda$, $\gamma$, $\sigma_0$, and $\tau$ using
the tuning procedure described in Section~\ref{sec:transport-parameter-tuning}.
Equations~\eqref{eq:transport-wind} and
\eqref{eq:transport-advection} represent wind-driven advection. Satellite
columns do not resolve the vertical distribution of plume mass, so
point-source methods commonly use an effective wind to approximate its mean
transport speed~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking,meier2024conversion}. We
approximate this wind by averaging the fields at the two endpoints
($\lambda=0.5$) and applying a modest speed correction ($\gamma=0.8$).
Equation~\eqref{eq:transport-advection} then applies semi-Lagrangian advection
by tracing each destination cell backward along the effective wind over
$\Delta t_i$ and bilinearly sampling the earlier raster at the resulting
departure location.

Equations~\eqref{eq:transport-diffusion-scale} and
\eqref{eq:transport-diffusion} approximate unresolved turbulent horizontal
dispersion. Gaussian plume representations are widely used in satellite
point-source studies~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking,meier2024conversion}. We
set $\sigma_0$ to one 3~km grid cell after one hour and increase the width with
$\sqrt{\Delta t_i}$. Applying the Gaussian
kernel to $A_i-B_i$ spreads the plume enhancement while preserving the
estimated background.

Equation~\eqref{eq:transport-decay} applies first-order loss to the
above-background enhancement. The effective timescale $\tau=8$~h represents
the combined persistence of the observed NO$_2$ plume under chemical removal
and other unresolved losses. Exponential loss is commonly used to describe the downwind decay of
satellite-observed \NOx{} plumes, whose effective lifetimes vary with
meteorology and plume chemistry~\cite{meier2024conversion}.
Equation~\eqref{eq:transport-residual} subtracts the resulting transport-only
prediction from the next observation.

Each five-observation sample produces four transport-residual rasters, one for
each pair of consecutive NO$_2$ rasters. For each pair, we advect and diffuse
the earlier observation's validity mask alongside its NO$_2$ raster, then
multiply it by the later observation's mask to form the residual's quality mask.
Figure~\ref{fig:sample-construction} shows all four residuals for the example
sample.

\subsection{Dataset Stratification}

Our split design has three goals: prevent leakage from overlapping AOIs,
produce an approximately 70\%/15\%/15\% stratification of samples, and construct class distributions that
support balanced training and comparable evaluation across the held-out
splits. We group overlapping AOIs into connected clusters and treat each
cluster as an indivisible assignment unit. Because each cluster contains an
arbitrary number of AOIs, samples, and class instances, cluster-level
assignment cannot generally meet every target exactly.

We use mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) to assign each
cluster to exactly one split while minimizing normalized deviations from
target counts of AOIs and eligible samples. The first optimization targets a
70\%/15\%/15\% sample stratification using AOI and sample counts,
after which we fix the training clusters. A second optimization assigns the
remaining clusters equally between validation and test while balancing AOI,
sample, and per-class counts. It also constrains both held-out splits to have
the same median number of plant units per AOI as the combined held-out pool.

We then uniformly downsample each training class to the size of the smallest
class. In validation and test, we retain every increase and decrease sample and
uniformly subsample steady samples to at most the size of the larger non-steady
class.

We then apply the raster filtering described in
Section~\ref{sec:sample-construction}. Across the three splits, 19.4\% of
selected decrease samples, 25.5\% of steady samples, and 32.4\% of increase
samples are retained through raster filtering. The retention pattern is
primarily driven by seasonal cloud cover: decrease samples occur more often in
winter, steady samples are less seasonally concentrated, and increase samples
occur more often in summer.
Table~\ref{tab:dataset-composition} reports the final dataset and class counts.

\subsection{ConvGRU Model}

Each sample supplies five $24\times24$ observation frames and four
transport-residual frames, one for each consecutive NO$_2$ transition. An
observation frame contains NO$_2$, its binary validity mask, eastward and northward
winds at 80~m, and air temperature at 2~m. A residual frame contains the transport
residual and an associated quality channel indicating support from valid observations. The model also receives four time-derived tabular
features computed at the sequence midpoint.

Separate convolutional encoders process the observation and residual frames,
sharing weights across time within each branch. A ConvGRU updates a spatial
hidden state over four transitions. The model then combines the final visual
representation with the time-derived features to classify emissions change
behavior. Figure~\ref{fig:convgru-architecture} shows the architecture and one
ConvGRU cell.

We denote the observation and residual encoders $E_x$ and $E_r$,
respectively, in Figure~\ref{fig:convgru-architecture}(a). Separate encoders
account for the different meanings of their inputs.
Both encoders begin with a $5\times5$ convolution that produces 32 channels,
followed by five residual blocks. Strided blocks increase the channel width
from 32 to 48 and then 64 while reducing each $24\times24$ raster to a
feature map with 64 channels and a spatial size of $6\times6$. Each residual block applies two $3\times3$
convolutions with group normalization, a SiLU activation after the first
convolution, and another SiLU activation after adding the skip connection.
Blocks that change channel width or spatial resolution use a $1\times1$
convolution on the skip path.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:convgru-architecture}(a), the encoded $t_0$
observation initializes the hidden state, so the model does not synthesize a
residual before the first observation. At each subsequent timestep $t_i$, the
model concatenates the encoded observation with the encoded residual for
$t_{i-1}\rightarrow t_i$. A $1\times1$ projection fuses the two encoded
feature maps before the recurrent update.

Figure~\ref{fig:convgru-architecture}(b) expands one recurrent update. Let
$\boldsymbol{f}_i$ denote the fused input and $\boldsymbol{h}_{i-1}$ the
previous hidden state, a $64\times6\times6$ feature map that carries spatial
information from earlier observations. The ConvGRU computes
\begin{align}
  [\boldsymbol{r}_i,\boldsymbol{z}_i]
    &= \sigma\!\left(W_g *
       [\boldsymbol{f}_i,\boldsymbol{h}_{i-1}]+\boldsymbol{b}_g\right), \\
  \widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_i
    &= \tanh\!\left(W_h *
       [\boldsymbol{f}_i,\boldsymbol{r}_i\odot\boldsymbol{h}_{i-1}]
       +\boldsymbol{b}_h\right), \\
  \boldsymbol{h}_i
    &= (1-\boldsymbol{z}_i)\odot\boldsymbol{h}_{i-1}
       +\boldsymbol{z}_i\odot\widetilde{\boldsymbol{h}}_i,
\end{align}
where the subscripts $g$ and $h$ denote the gate and candidate-state
convolutions, respectively. $W_g$ and $W_h$ are their learned $3\times3$
kernels, while $\boldsymbol{b}_g$ and $\boldsymbol{b}_h$ are the corresponding
biases. The symbol $*$ denotes convolution, and $[\cdot,\cdot]$ denotes
channel-wise concatenation. The reset gate
$\boldsymbol{r}_i$ controls how much previous spatial information contributes
to the candidate state, while the update gate $\boldsymbol{z}_i$ determines
how much of that candidate replaces the previous state.

After the fourth recurrent update, global average and maximum pooling summarize
distributed and localized evidence, respectively, in the final hidden state.
The model concatenates their outputs and projects them into a visual embedding.
A feedforward encoder maps four time-derived tabular features (the sine and
cosine of local solar hour and of day of year) into a tabular embedding. The
model concatenates the tabular embedding with the visual embedding after
recurrent processing, keeping the tabular features out of the spatial state updates. The fused representation passes
through a classification head to produce one logit for each emissions-change class:
increasing, steady, or decreasing.

\subsection{Baseline MLP Model}

We use a lightweight baseline MLP to gauge how much emissions change behavior
can be predicted from recurring daily and annual patterns without satellite or
weather inputs. It uses the same four time-derived tabular features as the
ConvGRU. The baseline MLP maps these features through 16- and 8-unit hidden
layers, each with layer normalization and a SiLU activation,
followed by a linear layer that produces logits for the three behavior classes.

\subsection{Transport Model Parameter Tuning}
\label{sec:transport-parameter-tuning}

We tuned the transport model on steady samples from the validation split to assess its ability to
predict NO$_2$ plume evolution during periods of relatively stable
\NOx{} emissions. We sampled 2,500 records and randomly selected one consecutive NO$_2$ raster transition
from each record.

We evaluated the wind-blending factor $\lambda$, wind-speed scale $\gamma$,
dispersion width $\sigma_0$, and decay timescale $\tau$ using
the L1 loss between the transport model's predicted NO$_2$ raster
$\widehat{N}_{i+1}$ and the observed raster $N_{i+1}$. The parameter set $\lambda=0.5$, $\gamma=0.8$,
$\sigma_0=1$, and $\tau=8$ yielded the lowest error from this procedure.

\subsection{Classification Performance}

We use F1 to summarize the balance between precision and recall for each
behavior class, and macro-F1 to give the three classes equal weight despite
unequal sample counts. Figure~\ref{fig:classification-performance} compares the
models on the test set. The ConvGRU achieves a macro-F1 of 0.56, compared with
0.45 for the baseline MLP.

ConvGRU achieved a training macro-F1 of 0.62 and a validation macro-F1 of 0.60,
compared with 0.53 and 0.53 for the baseline MLP. Its macro-F1 decreased by 0.06
from training to test, compared with a decrease of 0.08 for the MLP. On the test
set, the ConvGRU outperformed the baseline in F1 across all three classes, with
its largest advantage for steady samples: an absolute F1 difference of 0.14.
Decrease remained the ConvGRU's weakest class, with test recall of 0.47 versus
0.53 for the baseline MLP. Detecting decreases may be more difficult because
fewer decrease samples are available for training and previously emitted NO$_2$
can remain visible after emissions decline as the plume advects, disperses,
and decays.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

We retrain the ConvGRU for each ablation in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-study},
zeroing the listed input for every sample. For timestep ablations, we zero the
NO$_2$ and weather rasters but retain overlapping transport residuals. We omit
$t_0$ because it initializes the hidden state, making its removal different
from removing later observations.

Removing transport residuals causes the largest drop in test macro-F1 (12.58
percentage points), including a fall in steady F1 from 0.52 to 0.31. This
suggests the model uses deviations from predicted plume transport to
distinguish emissions changes from plume transport. Removing temperature costs
4.87 points and particularly hurts increase F1 (0.69 to 0.57). Temperature may
help the model interpret atmospheric effects in the rasters, although it could
also act as a proxy for electricity demand. Removing tabular features costs
only 0.74 points, supporting the view that the ConvGRU identifies emissions
trajectories primarily from visual inputs rather than seasonal timing alone.
Removing wind rasters costs only 0.27 points, but wind also enters the transport
residual calculation, so this does not establish that wind is uninformative.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Evaluation of Model Results}

The ConvGRU uses raster information about power plant activity beyond time of day
and seasonality. Its advantage over the baseline MLP is greatest for steady
samples, where features derived from time are less informative. Transport residuals
contribute most to the model's performance in the ablation study, particularly
for steady samples. This suggests that accounting for plume transport helps the
model distinguish emissions behavior from changes in atmospheric conditions.
The ConvGRU performs worse on decreases than on increases, possibly because
decreases are less common in the training set and previously emitted NO$_2$ can
remain visible after emissions fall.

Across the monthly, hourly, and regional subsets, both models tend to perform
better when increases are more prevalent than when decreases are more prevalent.
The ConvGRU's advantage tends to grow as the share of steady samples rises. A
binary task with classification labels of increase and non-increase might therefore be
easier to learn, since it would not require the model to distinguish decreases
from steady periods. Future work could test this framing during operating hours
when ramp-up activity is more common (e.g., in the morning) for a clearer visual signal across the dataset.

\subsection{Implications for Power Plant Monitoring}

Satellite plume inversion depends on observations that distinguish a plant's plume from nearby emissions.
Low source emissions, nearby sources, cloud screening, and very low or high wind speeds can reduce the number
of usable observations or increase uncertainty in emission-rate estimates~\cite{huang2026detectability,kuhlmann2026temporal,santaren2025benchmarking}.
Our model instead classifies relative emissions changes across 72~km $\times$ 72~km AOIs while accounting
for transport dynamics. This design could extend activity monitoring to some clustered or urban settings
where plume inversion is difficult, although we have not directly evaluated that comparison.

This modeling approach could support identification of power plant shutdowns and
restarts associated with planned maintenance, equipment failures, or periods when
units are available but not dispatched. Public access to individual generator
outage records varies across grid operators. For example, CAISO publishes daily
outage reports identifying individual generating resources and their unavailable
capacity. ERCOT publishes unplanned outage and derating records with a three-day
delay for individually identified generating resources, which can represent
individual units or groups of units. PJM publishes daily regional and system-wide
outage-capacity totals while generally keeping individual generator outages
confidential~\cite{caiso2026outages,ercot2026unplannedOutages,pjm2026dataAvailability}.
For facilities covered by EPA reporting programs, quarterly submissions of hourly
operating and emissions data provide a retrospective record of nonoperation and
subsequent restarts~\cite{epa2026powerSectorGuide,epa2026powerSectorData}. Our current
model predicts aggregate emissions changes across each AOI. An extension could
jointly predict the emissions-change class and a spatial contribution raster,
supervised using facility-level emissions changes derived from CEMS. This could
potentially help the model monitor emissions changes at the granularity required
to identify when a specific facility is undergoing shutdown or restart behavior.

Another potential application is monitoring substantial changes in generating
output from one or more units within an AOI. In response to dispatch instructions,
operating units may increase or decrease their output without an entire facility
shutting down or restarting. These adjustments can produce substantial emissions
changes even while other units in the AOI maintain their operating levels. Hourly
generation totals for balancing authority areas, including breakdowns by energy
source, are public, but timely output data for individual generators are less
consistently available. For example, PJM treats individual generator output as
confidential, while ERCOT releases resource-specific dispatch targets and
telemetered output with a 60-day delay~\cite{eia2026hourlyGrid,pjm2026dataAvailability,ercot2026dispatchDisclosure}.
EPA records also provide historical hourly generation information for many covered
units through quarterly reporting~\cite{epa2026powerSectorGuide,epa2026powerSectorData}.
The joint prediction of emissions trajectories and per-pixel spatial contributions
discussed above could also help identify facilities exhibiting behavior consistent
with substantial dispatch-related changes in generating output before corresponding
operational records become publicly available.

Growing electricity demand from data centers is driving the deployment of
dedicated natural gas power plants located at or near computing facilities. These
installations can comprise multiple gas turbines or natural gas-fired piston
engines connected to electrical generators. For example, SpaceXAI's Colossus 2 data
center draws power from a dedicated installation of natural gas turbines in
Southaven, Mississippi~\cite{eia2026dataCenterDemand,gauld2026dataCenter}. Some
dedicated data center power facilities may lack the publicly available hourly
emissions records provided by EPA's power sector reporting programs, creating an
opportunity for independent satellite monitoring~\cite{epa2026islandedGeneration}.
At facilities without publicly available CEMS records, our approach could provide
information about short-term emissions trajectories and associated operational
changes. Although ground-truth data for these facilities may be scarce, our model
could complement methods that estimate absolute emissions by providing additional
transparency into their short-term emissions activity.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}

The available TEMPO observation history limits the size and coverage of the modeling dataset.
Our observations span August 2023 through June 2026, which covers the full range of dates with both
TEMPO observations and quarterly CEMS data available at the time of study.
Cloud cover and pixel validity requirements further reduced the number of usable
five-observation sequences. As the TEMPO and EPA CEMS records grow, more sequences may meet our
sampling requirements. Beyond North America, GEMS observations could be paired with South Korea's publicly
released stack \NOx{} measurements, while Sentinel-4 observations could be paired with European generation
data for large individual units~\cite{yang2024gems,keco2026cleansys,esa2025sentinel4,entsoe2014transparency}.
These extensions would require new label construction and validation because the available ground data
differ from EPA CEMS. Improved NO$_2$ retrieval and cloud-correction algorithms may also make changes in
plume structure more discernible across our raster sequences~\cite{seo2024gemsRetrieval}.

Transport residuals produced the largest gain in our ablation study, but their calculation uses one effective
wind field, simplified horizontal dispersion, and a fixed decay timescale. A future model could interpolate
wind vector data based on stack height, account for boundary-layer mixing and plume rise, and model NO$_2$
chemistry as it varies with sunlight and temperature~\cite{huang2026detectability,meier2024conversion}.
Better predictions of how existing plumes move and persist could reduce transport-related variation in the
residuals, making emissions changes easier to detect, especially decreases obscured by lingering NO$_2$.

Our labels aggregate emissions from all facilities within each AOI. When nearby units change activity in
opposing directions, their signals can partly cancel, weakening visual evidence of the aggregate label.
The current model cannot identify which facility drove a predicted change or what caused it. Future work could
jointly predict the activity label and a per-pixel spatial contribution map to help associate the observed signal
with specific facilities in an AOI.

\section{Conclusion}

We formulate short-term power plant monitoring as detecting changes across sequences
of TEMPO NO$_2$ observations rather than estimating an exact emission rate from each scan. On more than 22,000 held-out samples covering 116 active U.S. plants,
the ConvGRU achieved a macro-F1 of 0.56, compared with 0.45 for a baseline using only time-derived features. This gain
suggests that the raster sequences contain information beyond recurring daily and seasonal patterns, even though retrieval
uncertainty, changing atmospheric conditions, and nearby sources can obscure individual plumes. Transport residuals compare
each scan with a prediction of how an existing plume should move and decay, and our ablation study identified them as the
most important model input. These findings suggest that change detection may be a more tractable framework for modeling
short-term emissions from noisy satellite observations than direct regression of absolute emissions. This approach
could support independent monitoring of plant activity during CEMS failures, before quarterly CEMS data are released
publicly, and at facilities not subject to hourly reporting requirements.
