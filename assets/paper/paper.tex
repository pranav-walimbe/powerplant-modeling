\begin{abstract}
NASA's Tropospheric Emissions: Monitoring of Pollution (TEMPO) instrument
provides hourly daytime observations of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide
(NO$_2$) across North America. These observations could support independent,
facility-level monitoring of power plant nitrogen oxides (\NOx{}) emissions,
filling gaps caused by continuous emissions monitoring system (CEMS) failures
and covering facilities not subject to hourly emissions-reporting requirements.
Previous studies have estimated hourly point-source emissions from individual
satellite scenes using empirical methods such as cross-sectional flux and the
directional derivative approach. However, these estimates are sensitive to
retrieval uncertainty, changing atmospheric transport, TEMPO's coarse spatial
resolution, signals from nearby emitters, and temporal mismatch with hourly
CEMS totals. We instead formulate facility monitoring as change detection over
sequences of TEMPO observations. This relative target uses repeated scans to
capture plume evolution while reducing sensitivity to persistent background
levels and biases in absolute column values. We develop a physics-guided
convolutional gated recurrent unit model (ConvGRU) that processes five consecutive
TEMPO observations, High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) meteorological fields,
and transport residuals. The network classifies the target power plant's
emissions as increasing, steady, or decreasing. On a held-out
evaluation set containing more than 20,000 samples from over 95 U.S. power
plants observed between August 2023 and July 2026, the model achieves a
classification macro-F1 score of 0.54, which outperformed a baseline MLP (0.49).
These results support the use of sequential TEMPO imagery for detecting short-term changes in power plant
emissions across facilities and observing conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Based on U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) inventory data, power plants
in the U.S. electricity-generation sector emitted approximately 0.74 million
short tons of \NOx{} in 2023, about 9.5\% of national
emissions~\cite{epa2026emissionsTrends}. \NOx{} emissions contribute to
ground-level ozone and secondary particulate matter, pollutants that can
inflame and damage the airways, reduce lung function, and aggravate
asthma~\cite{epa2016noxHealth}. EPA regulations require most large U.S.
fossil-fuel-fired generating units that produce electricity for sale to submit
unit-level hourly emissions data on a quarterly basis~\cite{epa2026powerMonitoring}.
However, monitoring equipment malfunctions and maintenance can make CEMS
measurements unavailable or invalid, requiring plants to report calculated
substitute values instead of direct measurements~\cite{epa2021substitute}.
Behind-the-meter generators that do not sell electricity can fall outside
federal hourly reporting requirements, as illustrated by the natural-gas
turbines powering SpaceXAI's Colossus 2 data center~\cite{gauld2026dataCenter}.
Outside the United States, comparable public hourly, unit-level emissions data
are generally unavailable, even where facilities continuously monitor
emissions~\cite{huang2026detectability}. Independent estimates could therefore
fill reporting gaps and reveal short-term changes before the EPA's quarterly release
of hourly emissions data.

TEMPO is an ultraviolet-visible spectrometer hosted aboard a geostationary satellite
that observes tropospheric vertical column densities (TVCDs) for various trace gases
across North America during daylight hours~\cite{zoogman2017tempo}. Earlier polar-orbiting
instruments, including the Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) and TROPOspheric Monitoring
Instrument (TROPOMI), typically observe a given location once per day; TEMPO's geostationary
view provides approximately hourly scans with finer spatial
resolution~\cite{levelt2006omi,veefkind2012tropomi,zoogman2017tempo}. Hourly sampling
captures intraday changes in power plant plumes and atmospheric transport that daily
observations can miss. However, power plant signals in TEMPO NO$_2$ TVCDs can be weakened
or obscured by factors including evolving meteorology and atmospheric chemistry, cloud cover,
viewing geometry and retrieval uncertainty, dilution within TEMPO's 2.0-by-4.75 km spatial resolution
per pixel, and interference from nearby emission sources~\cite{tempo2026userGuide,huang2026detectability,gauld2026dataCenter}.
Cross-comparisons with CEMS data, which is the most reliable ground truth available, introduce an
additional temporal mismatch because TEMPO observes a facility at a specific time during each hourly scan,
whereas CEMS data aggregate emissions over an entire clock hour~\cite{tempo2026userGuide,epa2026powerMonitoring}.

Satellite-based point-source studies typically combine observed column enhancements
with modeled atmospheric transport. Cross-sectional flux (CSF) integrates
background-subtracted columns across plume transects, while the directional
derivative approach (DDA) relates spatial column gradients to wind
fields~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking,sun2025hourly}. Santaren et al. benchmarked
five such methods on synthetic daily satellite scenes with known source rates and
found that cloud gaps and wind errors limited their accuracy and
coverage~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking}. For TEMPO, Sun et al. added hourly changes in the
observed NO$_2$ column to DDA and compared estimates for 14 U.S. power plants with
CEMS, but retrieval and chemical-conversion uncertainties produced substantial low
bias~\cite{sun2025hourly}. Beaudry et al. fit a rise--decay model to TEMPO plumes
from 64 plants and also found weaker performance for lower-emitting
sources~\cite{beaudry2026quantification}.

Machine learning offers an alternative by learning emission-related plume patterns
from data. Huang and Wang predicted
whether TROPOMI could detect power plant NO$_2$ plumes from plant, meteorological,
surface, and sensor variables rather than estimating emissions from the
imagery~\cite{huang2026detectability}. Alnaim et al. used recurrent models with TROPOMI,
meteorological, and EPA data to estimate power plant emissions, but did not test
transfer to previously unseen plants~\cite{alnaim2022evaluating}. Regression has
also been demonstrated for other gases: U-Plume combines learned methane plume masks
with imagery and wind to infer source rates, while Dumont et al. trained a CNN on
simulated CO$_2$ and wind fields to estimate power plant
emissions~\cite{bruno2024uplume,dumont2024deepLearning}. To our knowledge, no published study
has used sequences of hourly TEMPO NO$_2$ imagery to infer facility-level emissions
or behavior.

Estimating absolute hourly emissions from individual TEMPO scenes requires
separating weak facility signals from background pollution and resolving
uncertainties in retrieval, atmospheric chemistry, and transport. We instead
use TEMPO's hourly sampling to detect relative changes over short observation
sequences, reducing dependence on absolute column values and persistent
background levels.

Our contributions are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We formulate satellite-based power plant monitoring as a three-class
    change-detection task that identifies increasing, steady, and decreasing
    emissions behavior.

    \item We introduce a physics-guided deep learning framework that combines
    sequences of TEMPO NO$_2$ observations with meteorology and transport
    information to predict emissions behavior.

    \item We evaluate the model on held-out power plants across geographic
    regions, seasons, and operating profiles and compare its performance with
    the temporal MLP.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Sources}

\subsection{TEMPO NO\texorpdfstring{$_2$}{2} Data}

NASA distributes TEMPO NO$_2$ retrievals in two formats: Level 2 data on the
instrument's native measurement footprints and Level 3 data regridded from each
east-to-west scan~\cite{tempo2026userGuide}. We downloaded version 4 Level 2
NO$_2$ retrievals from August 2023 through August 2026. Along with NO$_2$
TVCDs, the product provides metadata including native footprint geometry,
mirror-step observation times, main data quality flags, and effective cloud
fractions, which we later use for regridding, quality filtering, and temporal
alignment with CEMS records.

\subsection{CEMS Records}

We downloaded hourly emissions records for individual power plant units and
annual facility and unit attributes from the U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency's Clean Air Markets Program Data (CAMPD) APIs for August 2023 through
September 2026. From the hourly records, we use facility and unit identifiers,
reporting date and hour, \NOx{} mass and its measurement flag, operating time,
and primary fuel. From the annual attributes, we use facility coordinates and
unit-level primary fuel.

\subsection{HRRR Weather Data}

The High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) is an hourly weather model with a
3~km grid covering the contiguous United States. From NOAA's public HRRR
archive, we downloaded hourly 2~m air temperature and eastward and northward
wind components at 80~m above ground for August 2023 through August 2026.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{AOI Construction}

For each power plant, we define a 72~km $\times$ 72~km area of interest (AOI)
centered on the facility and divide it into a $24 \times 24$ grid of
3~km $\times$ 3~km cells. For each AOI-hour, we sum the unit-level \NOx{} mass
reported by all facilities in the AOI and discard the hour if any contributing
measurement is invalid or unavailable. We score each AOI using its median total
\NOx{} mass across hours whose mean unit operating time meets or exceeds the
AOI-specific median. We retain the highest-scoring 50\% to focus the dataset on
emissions signals more likely to be detected by TEMPO.

\subsection{TEMPO Regridding and Quality Filtering}

TEMPO Level 2 observations lie on irregular native footprints, whereas our
model uses a fixed spatial grid. We project the footprint boundaries and AOI
grid to the contiguous-U.S. Albers equal-area coordinate system. We then assign
each AOI grid cell an NO$_2$ TVCD by averaging the TEMPO footprints that
intersect it, weighted by their area of overlap. Figure~\ref{fig:tempoRegridding}
illustrates this mapping from native footprints to the fixed AOI grid.

A footprint contributes to the NO$_2$ average only when its main data quality
flag equals zero and its effective cloud fraction does not exceed 0.20. Grid
cells without an accepted footprint overlap remain masked, and we retain a
binary mask of the original valid cells. We fill the masked NO$_2$ cells by
bilinear interpolation before later processing.

\subsection{Sample Construction}

Each sample contains five consecutive observations of one AOI, indexed from
$t_0$ to $t_4$. We retain a sequence only when each pair of adjacent TEMPO
scans is separated by 40--70 minutes and every NO$_2$ raster has valid pixel coverage of at
least 85\%. At each timestep, we pair the TEMPO raster with
the HRRR temperature and wind rasters from the closest hour interpolated to a common
$24 \times 24$ raster size and spatial coverage as the NO$_2$ rasters.

Because CEMS records correspond to clock hours rather than TEMPO scan times,
we linearly interpolate the AOI-hour \NOx{} emissions between the surrounding
hourly CEMS records at each scan time. Let $E_i$ denote the interpolated emissions
at time $t_i$. For scans $t_a$ through $t_b$, we calculate the time-weighted mean
\begin{equation}
    \overline{E}_{a:b} =
    \frac{\sum_{i=a}^{b-1}(t_{i+1}-t_i)(E_i+E_{i+1})/2}
    {t_b-t_a}.
\end{equation}
We define the sequence change as
$\Delta E=\overline{E}_{2:4}-\overline{E}_{0:2}$ and use the threshold
$\theta=250~\mathrm{lb\,h^{-1}}$ to assign its category:
\begin{equation}
    c =
    \begin{cases}
        \text{increase}, & \Delta E \geq \theta, \\
        \text{steady}, & -\theta < \Delta E < \theta, \\
        \text{decrease}, & \Delta E \leq -\theta.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Transport Model}

We also supply the model with rasters that use a transport model to separate
new NO$_2$ emissions from existing plume at a given timestep.
For each interval $\Delta t_i=t_{i+1}-t_i$, let $N_i(\boldsymbol{x})$ denote
the earlier NO$_2$ raster, $\boldsymbol{u}_i(\boldsymbol{x})$ its wind field,
and $B_i$ the median NO$_2$ value along the raster boundary. We approximate
transport as

\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \overline{\boldsymbol{u}}_i
      &= \gamma\!\left[(1-\lambda)\boldsymbol{u}_i
         + \lambda\boldsymbol{u}_{i+1}\right], \\
    A_i(\boldsymbol{x})
      &= N_i\!\left(\boldsymbol{x}
         -\overline{\boldsymbol{u}}_i(\boldsymbol{x})\Delta t_i\right), \\
    \sigma_i
      &= \sigma_0\sqrt{\frac{\Delta t_i}{1~\mathrm{h}}}, \\
    D_i
      &= B_i+G_{\sigma_i}*(A_i-B_i), \\
    \widehat{N}_{i+1}
      &= B_i+\exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta t_i}{\tau}\right)(D_i-B_i), \\
    R_i
      &= N_{i+1}-\widehat{N}_{i+1}.
  \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The semi-Lagrangian step backtraces each destination cell and uses bilinear
sampling to obtain the advected field $A_i$. Convolution with the Gaussian
kernel $G_{\sigma_i}$ disperses the enhancement above the boundary background,
and the exponential term applies effective NO$_2$ decay with lifetime $\tau$.
The parameters $\gamma$, $\lambda$, $\sigma_0$, and $\tau$ control wind-speed
scaling, endpoint-wind blending, dispersion, and decay; we select them using
validation data as part of the model optimization procedure described later.

We compute one residual $R_i$ for each adjacent pair, producing four residual
rasters per sample. We transport the earlier validity mask through the same
advection and dispersion steps and combine it with the later mask to identify
unreliable residual pixels. Figure~\ref{fig:transport-residuals} shows the four
residuals for an example sequence.

\subsection{Dataset Stratification}

Our split design has three goals: prevent leakage from overlapping AOIs,
approximate a 70\%/15\%/15\% division, and distribute the three classes across
the splits while limiting class imbalance. We group overlapping AOIs into
connected clusters and treat each cluster as an indivisible assignment unit.
Because each cluster contains an arbitrary number of AOIs, samples, and class
instances, cluster-level assignment cannot generally meet every target exactly.
Steady samples account for $\sim90\%$ of the raw sample pool, compared with
$\sim7\%$ increase and $\sim3\%$ decrease samples. Although we later use a class-weighted
training loss, we control the composition of each split so that steady behavior
does not dominate training or evaluation.

We use mixed-integer programming to assign each cluster to exactly one split
while minimizing normalized deviations from the target AOI and sample counts.
The first optimization targets the 70\%/15\%/15\% division and fixes the
resulting training clusters. A second optimization divides the remaining
clusters equally between validation and test while balancing their AOI, sample,
and per-class counts. We then downsample each training class to the size of the
smallest class. In validation and test, we retain every increase and decrease
sample and uniformly sample steady examples until their count matches that of
the more common change class.

\subsection{ConvGRU Model}

  \label{fig:convgru-architecture}

We use a convolutional recurrent neural network with a convolutional gated
recurrent unit (ConvGRU) to preserve spatial structure while combining the five
observations in each sample. At each timestep, an
observation encoder processes NO$_2$, the NO$_2$ quality mask, temperature, and
the two wind components. A separate encoder processes each of the four
transport residuals together with its quality mask. Each encoder shares weights
across time and reduces the $24 \times 24$ inputs to spatial feature maps for
temporal aggregation. Figure~\ref{fig:convgru-architecture} summarizes the
architecture.

The encoded first observation initializes the recurrent hidden state. At each
later timestep, the model concatenates the current observation features with
the transport residual from the preceding interval, fuses them with a
$1 \times 1$ convolution, and updates the hidden state with the ConvGRU. Its
convolutional gates retain the spatial arrangement of plume features while
learning how much information to preserve or replace across the sequence. This
design lets the network use both the observed atmospheric state and the portion
of each change that the transport model does not explain.

The final recurrent feature map feeds the classification head. We combine
global average and maximum pooling to form a visual embedding. A
small feedforward network separately encodes the four temporal features, and its output is
concatenated with the visual embedding before the classification head produces
logits for the increase, steady, and decrease classes. This late-fusion design
keeps the tabular context separate while the ConvGRU learns the raster sequence.

\subsection{Temporal MLP}

The temporal MLP measures how much emissions-change
behavior can be predicted from recurring daily and annual patterns without
satellite or weather inputs. We represent each sample using
four cyclic features: the sine and cosine of local solar hour (midpoint of timesteps) and the sine and
cosine of day of year. The temporal MLP maps these features through hidden layers of 16
and 8 units, each followed by layer normalization and a SiLU activation. A
linear output layer produces logits for the three behavior classes.
Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-mlp} shows this architecture.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Classification Performance}

We evaluate classification using macro-F1 and balanced accuracy to account for
differences in class prevalence. On the test set, the ConvGRU achieves a
macro-F1 of 0.538 and balanced accuracy of 0.553, compared with 0.494 and 0.513
for the temporal MLP. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:classification-performance},
the ConvGRU improves decrease and steady classification, while its increase
scores are lower than the temporal MLP's on the test split. Results across the training,
validation, and test splits show the same aggregate advantage for the ConvGRU.
