\begin{abstract}
NASA's geostationary Tropospheric Emissions: Monitoring of Pollution (TEMPO)
instrument provides hourly observations of tropospheric nitrogen dioxide
(NO$_2$) across North America. Reliable satellite-based estimates of power plant nitrogen oxides (\NOx{}) emissions
from these observations could fill gaps in reported data caused by continuous emissions
monitoring system (CEMS) failures and provide independent estimates for facilities
not subject to hourly emissions-reporting requirements. Previous studies have estimated hourly
point-source \NOx{} emissions using satellite-based empirical methods, such as cross-sectional
flux and the directional derivative approach. However, these methods struggle to generalize
across many observations due to varying atmospheric conditions, coarse spatial resolution of
TEMPO observations, and confounding signals from nearby emitting sources. We formulate the problem
as change detection over sequences of TEMPO observations, allowing the model to learn plume evolution
while external confounders are near-constant. Our convolutional recurrent neural network (CRNN) processes
five consecutive TEMPO observations, High-Resolution Rapid Refresh (HRRR) meteorological fields, and
physics-guided transport residuals to jointly classify plant emissions as increasing, steady, or decreasing
and predict class-conditioned spatial contribution rasters. On a held-out evaluation set covering more than
15,000 emission sequences from over 100 power plants across the United States from August 2023 to July 2026, our model improved
classification accuracy by X\% over a cross-sectional flux (CSF) baseline and Y\% over a
seasoanlity-only basline, suggesting our model posseses stronger generalization capabilities
for detecting changes in power plant emissions behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Based on U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) inventory data, power plants
in the U.S. electricity-generation sector emitted approximately 0.74 million
short tons of \NOx{} in 2023, about 9.5\% of national
emissions~\cite{epa2026emissionsTrends}. \NOx{} emissions contribute to
ground-level ozone and secondary particulate matter, pollutants that can
inflame and damage the airways, reduce lung function, and aggravate
asthma~\cite{epa2016noxHealth}. EPA regulations require most large U.S.
fossil-fuel-fired generating units that produce electricity for sale to submit
unit-level hourly emissions data on a quarterly basis~\cite{epa2026powerMonitoring}.
However, monitoring equipment malfunctions and maintenance can make CEMS
measurements unavailable or invalid, requiring plants to report calculated
substitute values instead of direct measurements~\cite{epa2021substitute}.
Behind-the-meter generators that do not sell electricity can fall outside
federal hourly reporting requirements, as illustrated by the natural-gas
turbines powering SpaceXAI's Colossus 2 data center~\cite{gauld2026dataCenter}.
Outside the United States, comparable public hourly, unit-level emissions data
are generally unavailable, even where facilities continuously monitor
emissions~\cite{huang2026detectability}. Independent estimates could therefore
fill reporting gaps and reveal short-term changes before the EPA's quarterly release
of hourly emissions data.

TEMPO is an ultraviolet-visible spectrometer hosted aboard a geostationary satellite
that observes tropospheric vertical column densities (TVCDs) for various trace gases
across North America during daylight hours~\cite{zoogman2017tempo}. Earlier polar-orbiting
instruments, including the Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) and TROPOspheric Monitoring
Instrument (TROPOMI), typically observe a given location once per day; TEMPO's geostationary
view provides approximately hourly scans with finer spatial
resolution~\cite{levelt2006omi,veefkind2012tropomi,zoogman2017tempo}. Hourly sampling
captures intraday changes in power plant plumes and atmospheric transport that daily
observations can miss. However, power plant signals in TEMPO NO$_2$ TVCDs can be weakened
or obscured by factors including evolving meteorology and atmospheric chemistry, cloud cover,
viewing geometry and retrieval uncertainty, dilution within TEMPO's 2.0-by-4.75 km spatial resolution
per pixel, and interference from nearby emission sources~\cite{tempo2025userGuide,huang2026detectability,gauld2026dataCenter}.
Cross-comparisons with CEMS data, which is the most reliable ground truth available, introduce an
additional temporal mismatch because TEMPO observes a facility at a specific time during each hourly scan,
whereas CEMS data aggregate emissions over an entire clock hour~\cite{tempo2025userGuide,epa2026powerMonitoring}.

Satellite-based point-source studies typically estimate emissions by relating an
observed column enhancement to atmospheric transport. The cross-sectional flux
(CSF) method integrates background-subtracted columns across plume transects and
multiplies the resulting line density by an effective wind speed, whereas the
integrated mass enhancement (IME) method converts the total excess plume mass to
a source rate using an estimated residence time~\cite{meier2024conversion,santaren2025benchmarking}.
Gaussian plume methods instead fit a parameterized dispersion model to the observed
enhancement~\cite{santaren2025benchmarking}. Flux divergence and directional
derivative approaches (DDA) apply mass-conservation relationships to satellite
column fields and meteorological data without first fitting a plume shape. These
approaches have been applied to TROPOMI NO$_2$ observations to estimate point-source
\NOx{} emissions~\cite{meier2024conversion,sun2025hourly}. Sun et al. extended DDA
with a column-tendency term and used TEMPO observations to derive hourly estimates
for 14 U.S. power plants~\cite{sun2025hourly}. Machine-learning studies of satellite
NO$_2$ have used convolutional neural networks to identify TROPOMI plumes and
classifiers to predict whether power-plant plumes are detectable under given
meteorological, surface, and viewing conditions~\cite{finch2022automated,huang2026detectability}.
These studies identify when a plume is visible but do not infer hourly plant
emissions from sequences of NO$_2$ imagery. For other trace gases, learned methods
have progressed from plume segmentation to source-rate estimation, including a
U-Net and CNN pipeline for methane and CNN models trained to estimate power-plant
CO$_2$ emissions from simulated satellite scenes~\cite{bruno2024uplume,dumont2024deepLearning}.
